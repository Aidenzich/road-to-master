## Introduction

Video inpainting and object removal must fill the masked region with content that "looks real, is semantically correct, and is stable across frames" — for example, removing a person or subtitles from an advertisement video and reconstructing the clean background. There have historically been two main routes. The first is flow-guided propagation, exemplified by ProPainter: first perform recurrent flow completion to estimate optical flow, then use dual-domain propagation to move "known pixels that once appeared" along the flow into masked frames, and finally use a mask-guided sparse Transformer to generate the unknown pixels that never appeared. The second is video Transformers, such as STTN and FuseFormer, which bring ViT into the spatio-temporal dimensions. The common weakness of both routes is that when the mask is large, propagation cannot find known pixels to move, and the generative capacity of their synthesis module (GAN or sparse Transformer) is insufficient, leaving obvious blur and mosaic artifacts.

Recent diffusion models have improved generative power, but each comes at a cost. DiffuEraser uses Stable Diffusion as the inpainting backbone and initializes with ProPainter's output as a prior, yet limited by its video-generation capacity, texture naturalness is still insufficient, with common regular texture artifacts and blur; MiniMax-Remover takes a streamlined two-stage route but takes more than 7 minutes to infer a single 2K clip. Meanwhile, text-to-video Diffusion Transformers (DiT) — CogVideoX, HunyuanVideo, LTX-Video — replace separated temporal/spatial attention with 3D full attention, and compress along time and space together with a 3D VAE, greatly improving consistency and flicker under large motion. EraserDiT's core claim is exactly this: to bring the strong generative power and spatio-temporal consistency of such video DiTs to the "large-hole, large-motion, high-resolution (claimed up to 1080p)" object-removal task.

The authors list four contributions. First, introduce a DiT that uses 3D full attention to maintain the spatio-temporal consistency of high-resolution video during object removal, and rely on the high compression ratio of a 3D Causal VAE to handle up to 1080p. Second, propose an inference-stage Circular Position-Shift (CPS) strategy to strengthen temporal consistency of long clips. Third, to train the removal of large dynamic objects (people, animals), the authors build about 60,000 mask videos. Fourth, propose a method to automatically generate a text prompt so that the prompt describes the scene "after the target is removed." The paper's most eye-catching efficiency figure is: on a single NVIDIA H800, without any acceleration method, processing a 97-frame high-resolution video takes only 65 seconds.

How the paper validates whether the method works is worth stating up front: evaluation uses the two datasets DAVIS and HQVI, the quantitative metrics are PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, VFID (four in total) plus inference time, and the baselines only compare the two recent diffusion methods DiffEraser and MiniMax-Remover; qualitative comparisons and dynamic evidence of temporal consistency are placed in videos on the project page. The mask sources of the two datasets are not the same: on DAVIS the authors generate binary masks themselves and reconstruct the masked video accordingly, while on HQVI they directly use the ground-truth masks provided by the dataset — this difference is worth keeping in mind when interpreting cross-dataset numbers (especially for DAVIS with its self-generated masks). Overall this is a concise technical report presented in engineering-report form; the main text has no user study, and no quantitative ablation of the flagship contribution CPS (see Critical Assessment).

## First Principles

### Backbone choice: why LTX-Video

EraserDiT is not a DiT trained from scratch; instead it takes Lightricks' LTX-Video as the base architecture and fine-tunes it. The authors' trade-off is straightforward: HunyuanVideo has the best generation quality but needs 60GB VRAM and is slow to infer at 720p, 129 frames; LTX-Video reaches about 1:192 compression with a $32\times32\times8$ spatio-temporal downsampling, buying extremely fast inference, and although its pure text-to-video quality is not as good as HunyuanVideo, video inpainting is a "strongly-referenced" task (most pixels come from the original clip) with lower demand on generative power, so the speed-first LTX-Video is a worthwhile choice. Across the whole pipeline, the 3D VAE and text encoder (T5) are frozen, and only all parameters of the denoising video transformer (DiT) are fine-tuned; the three submodules loaded by the official `inference_utils.py` — `AutoencoderKLLTXVideo`, `T5EncoderModel`, `LTXVideoTransformer3DModel` — together with `FlowMatchEulerDiscreteScheduler` correspond exactly to this design.

![Figure 2 (paper Figure 2, training pipeline): the prompt passes through the text encoder (frozen), and the masked video passes through the 3D VAE Enc (frozen) to obtain a latent, which is concatenated along the channel dimension (C) with the mask sequence and noise, and fed into DiT×n (RMSNorm→Self-attention→Cross-attention→RMSNorm→FFN; only this block is trainable, marked with a flame in the figure); text is injected via cross-attention, and finally the 3D VAE Dec (frozen) decodes the video with the object removed.](imgs/train_pipeline.png)

### How the input is constructed and how conditioning is fed

During training, a background video $V_i$ and a mask video $M_j$ are randomly sampled, first multiplied pixel-wise to obtain the masked video $V_M = V_i \ast M_j$ (masked regions zeroed out), then encoded by the 3D VAE encoder at $32\times32\times8$ into a latent; the corresponding mask sequence is also downsampled to the same scale. The input of the denoising transformer is formed by concatenating the "noised latent," the "masked-video latent," and the "downsampled mask" along the channel dimension; the prompt is encoded by T5 and fused with the video tokens via cross-attention. The official `pipeline_ltx_video2video.py` confirms this conditioning structure: at each step the transformer simultaneously takes `hidden_states` (noisy latent), `cond_latents` (masked-video latent), and `mask_values` (mask), and `num_channels_latents = (in_channels - 1) // 2` corresponds exactly to the "two latents + one mask" channel configuration. Notably, official inference is actually video-to-video: it noises the masked-video latent with `strength=0.8` as the starting point, rather than generating from pure Gaussian noise.

Because the 3D Causal VAE also compresses 8× in the temporal dimension, $n$ pixel frames are compressed into a latent temporal length of

$$
f_{\text{lat}} = 1 + \frac{n-1}{8}
$$

that is, "the first frame is independent, and every subsequent 8 frames are merged into 1 latent frame." This brings a mask-alignment detail: every 8 consecutive mask frames must be merged into 1 mask. The official `pre.py`'s `mask_video_nchw` takes the "union" over these 8 frames (`torch.sum(...) >= 1 → 255`, marking as masked if any single frame is masked), which is the correct approach; yet the paper text writes "intersected" (intersection), a wording–code inconsistency — an intersection would miss masks that appear in only some frames. The code additionally first applies 9 cross-kernel dilations to the mask (`dilate_iter=9`, `ksize=(9,9)`) before compressing. Taking the paper's flagship test of 97 frames as an example, the non-padded formula gives $1+(97-1)/8 = 13$ latent frames, while the 81 frames used in training compress to 11; but note that this 13 is only the paper-level formula value — the released inference code first reflect-pads the clip to `TEMP_INFER_LEN=121` before feeding the DiT, so the actual latent temporal length is 16 rather than 13 (derivation in the later section "One concrete forward pass").

### Inference flow and automatic prompt

At inference, the user only needs to draw a bounding box around the object to be removed on the first frame; the role of this box is to condition a VLM (the paper says "e.g. Qwen2.5-VL") to automatically generate a prompt "describing the scene after the target is removed." Note that the mask sequence $M_{test}$ is obtained in a separate step; the paper only writes "based on the prompt … a mask sequence $M_{test}$ can be obtained," and does not explain the actual mask-generation method — that is, "producing the mask directly from the box" is not written out in the paper, and this step is left blank in the main text. After obtaining the mask, it is multiplied pixel-wise with the original clip to get the masked video, and the three are fed together into EraserDiT to produce the output. In the released code this gap is even more evident: `inference.py` does not automatically produce a mask at all; it must be given a mask video externally by the user — the argument `--mask_path` (default `data/10268234_mask.mp4`) is a required input, and `run_batch` passes that video into the preprocessing as `mask_path=video_mask_path`; conversely `--bbox_path` defaults to `None` and is only used when the resolution exceeds about 1080p and a ROI must be cropped. As for "automatic prompt generation," it is also not truly productized yet: the released `img_caption.py` actually uses MiniCPM-o-2.6 (not the Qwen2.5-VL stated in the paper), and it is a standalone demo script carrying the author's local absolute path and hardcoding "Remove the woman"; it is not wired into `inference.py` (whose `--prompt` must be given manually by the user, with default value "There is a bridge over the lake.").

### Circular Position-Shift: paper description vs. released implementation

CPS targets the scenario where "the inference clip length exceeds the maximum training length." The paper's approach (Algorithm 1, Figure 4) is: first, using reflect mode, reflect-pad a sequence $[0,l]$ of length $l$ with $[l-1,1]$, concatenating it into a circular sequence, with ring length

$$
L = 2l - 2
$$

On the circular sequence, any continuous subsequence is a physically coherent segment of video. Then set a cumulative shift $\alpha_\sigma$, adding $\alpha$ at each denoising timestep, so that a sliding window of length $f$ enters the sequence from a different position at different timesteps, thereby repeatedly "smoothing over" the seams at clip boundaries. Because sliding inference nearly doubles the cost, the authors further use CFG distillation to distill the "unconditional + conditional" two-path outputs into a single student, trained under a fixed guidance scale $3.0$, bringing the cost back to the original level.

![Figure 4 (paper Figure 4, Circular Position-Shift): the top row is the original sequence frame 0→n; the middle row, after reflect padding, appends the reversed sequence frame n-1→1, forming a ring (purple arrows indicate the head and tail joining); the green, yellow, black, and red boxes in the bottom row are sliding windows entering at different positions, and the rightmost red dashed box crosses the tail of the ring back to the start, illustrating "circular" and "shift."](imgs/cycle.png)

However, after statically inspecting the released code, I could not find, in the single-GPU inference path, the kind of implementation from Algorithm 1 where "each timestep cumulatively adds $\alpha_\sigma$ and the window slides." The long-clip mechanism I could locate is a different, more naive streaming: `inference.py` processes batch by batch in units of `TEMP_INFER_LEN=121`, keeping a `shift_alpha = 1*8+1 = 9` frame overlap between batches (`pre_video_shift = output_frames[-9:]` connected to the start of the next batch); when the last batch is short, it is padded using `pre.py`'s `flip(0)[1:-1]` (reflect); each batch itself is a full-attention vid2vid denoising over 121 pixel frames — note that `TEMP_INFER_LEN=121` is the pixel-frame batch length of preprocessing, not the latent length; by the $1+(n-1)/8$ rule, 121 pixel frames correspond to $1+(121-1)/8 = 16$ temporal latent frames. Moreover, the released denoising loop actually runs full dual-path CFG (two transformer forwards per step, `guidance_scale=3`), rather than the "CFG-distilled single student" that the paper says is used to offset the doubled cost. Therefore CPS, this flagship contribution, is presented in the public code as "fixed 9-frame overlap sliding-window streaming + tail reflect padding," which is not formally identical to the step-by-step circular shift of the paper's Algorithm 1 — this is a gap in reproducibility.

### High resolution is actually achieved by "cropping"

The paper title and abstract tout 2K / high resolution, but the implementation reveals a key premise: in `inference.py`, `crop_flag = True if height*width > 1088*1920 else False`; once the video exceeds about 2K, cropping is triggered. `pre.py`'s `bbox_cal` does not read any mask frame or mask video; instead it opens the CSV pointed to by `--bbox_path` (whose help string is mislabeled "Input mask path"), parses its `bboxes` column row by row, takes the `inference_idx`-th bounding box of each row, and after collecting them computes the four coordinate extremes (`x1_min`, `x2_max`, `y1_min`, `y2_max`), then centers on these extremes to crop a $1920\times1088$ (or $1088\times1920$ for portrait) window; by the code logic, if the horizontal total span of these boxes exceeds the window (`x2_max - x1_min > crop_w`, or likewise in the height direction), `bbox_cal` returns `None`, and the main program outputs "The maximum mask movement in the current video sequence exceeds 1080 × 1920" and gives up. However, this is "design intent" rather than actual execution behavior I can verify from the released code: `inference.py:52` calls `preprocessor.bbox_video(height, width, bbox_path)` with only 3 arguments, but `pre.py:75`'s `bbox_video` also requires a 4th parameter `inference_idx`; as released, the high-resolution (cropping) branch would first raise a `TypeError` on this call and never reach the above movement check. So what is described here is a call incompatibility found in the static code, and readers should not take "graceful giving up" as a verified execution result. In any case, by design the DiT never performs full attention over the complete 2160×2100; instead it first crops to a window around the object at $\le 1088\times1920$ (essentially ≤1080p), generates within that window, and pastes back into the original image. This explains the apparent contradiction "the contribution says up to 1080p, yet the abstract says 2160×2100": the model's generation-resolution ceiling is 1080p, and higher resolution is merely handled by cropping. The cost is restricted use cases — the removed object, together with its motion trajectory, must lie entirely within a single 1088×1920 box.

### Training, data, and efficiency settings (overview)

The table below organizes the settings and cost numbers from the paper and the code; note these are "settings and costs," not evaluation results.

| Aspect | Setting | Concrete numbers / source |
|-|-|-|
| Backbone | LTX-Video fine-tuning, only train the video transformer | 3D VAE, T5 frozen |
| Training resolution / frames | fixed resolution, fixed frame count | $960\times960$, 81 frames |
| Iterations / hardware | Adam-W, Rectified Flow | 280k iters, 24× A100(80G), lr $3\times10^{-5}$ |
| Loss (two stages) | L2 for the first 150k; then switch to Focal Area | see formula below |
| frame sampling step | random sampling step during training to strengthen large motion | 1–6 |
| Background video | diverse backgrounds downloaded from Pexels | 600,000 clips |
| mask data | SA-V + self-built animal/person masks (extracted with SAM 2 / Grounded SAM 2) | animals ~30,000 (1–5s) + people ~32,000 (5–20s) ≈ 60,000 |
| Inference steps | official `num_inference_steps=50` × `strength=0.8` | effective 40 steps |
| Inference clip length | `TEMP_INFER_LEN` | 121 frames (overlap 9) |
| Flagship efficiency | 2160×1200, 97 frames, H800, no acceleration | 65 seconds (needs >60GB VRAM) |

The second-stage Focal Area loss focuses the loss on the mask region:

$$
L_{\text{focal}} = L_2 \cdot (1 + D_{\text{mask}})
$$

where $D_{\text{mask}}$ is the dilated mask of $M_j^{input}$, equivalent to weighting the interior of the mask (where content is to be generated), making the model work harder to learn the reconstruction of the removed region.

### One concrete forward pass: how the flagship efficiency test runs

Taking the efficiency test of the paper's Table 1 as an example, let us walk through its "by-design" data flow (as noted in the previous section, the cropping branch of the released code raises a `TypeError` first due to argument incompatibility, so what follows describes the design intent rather than a directly runnable path): input a 2160×1200, 97-frame video. Because $2160\times1200 > 1088\times1920$, cropping is triggered; by the extremes of the per-row `bboxes` coordinates in the `bbox_path` CSV (not any mask frame), a $1920\times1088$ window is cropped; after aligning to a multiple of 32, spatial 32× downsampling gives an approximately $60\times34$ latent grid. The temporal dimension must be viewed in two layers: the paper-level non-padded formula gives $1+(97-1)/8 = 13$ latent frames, but the released code's streaming preprocessing does not directly feed 97 frames — `pre.py`'s first-batch path (`batch_idx==0`), by `TEMP_INFER_LEN=121`, `shift_alpha=9`, computes that it is still $121-97 = 24$ frames short, and so reflect-pads (`flip(0)[1:-1]`) up to 121 frames; `inference_utils.py` then calls the pipeline with `num_frames=videos.shape[0]=121`, and `pipeline_ltx_video2video.py` obtains 16 temporal latent frames by $(121-1)//8+1 = 16$. Therefore, in the released path, the latent the DiT actually processes is about $[1, C, 16, 34, 60]$ (the temporal dimension is 16, coming from the padded 121, not 13 from the non-padded formula). On the conditioning side, after concatenating the masked-video latent and downsampled mask along the channel dimension, it noises with `strength=0.8` as the start, runs 40 steps of rectified-flow denoising, doing `guidance_scale=3` dual-path CFG at each step; after decoding back to pixels, it is pasted back into the original 2160×1200 frame. The whole thing takes about 65 seconds on a single H800, compared to MiniMax-Remover, which uses 6 steps on the same input but takes 7 minutes 20 seconds — EraserDiT's speed advantage comes mainly from LTX-Video's high-compression backbone, not from fewer denoising steps.

## 🧪 Critical Assessment

### The problem is real, but this is an engineering-flavored technical report

Video removal of subtitles, logos, passers-by, and animals is a real and high-frequency need in media and short-video production lines (the authors are from Mango TV, a reasonable background). The method makes pragmatic trade-offs in backbone selection, data engineering, and inference engineering. But it must be stressed: this is a concise technical report; the main text lacks quantitative validation of its flagship contribution and rigorous comparison design, examined point by point below.

### The flagship number contradicts itself, and the "no acceleration" claim is debatable

The same efficiency selling point does not line up within the paper itself: the abstract and official README both write 2160×2100, 97 frames, 65 seconds, but the Table 1 caption and main text write 2160×1200, 97 frames, 65 seconds (an earlier caption version in the source file is even 2160×1200, 121 frames). The flagship-resolution number is inconsistent between the abstract and the table, and readers cannot tell which is the actual measured condition. Second, the abstract claims "without any acceleration method," yet the paper itself describes CFG distillation as a cost-reduction means, while the released code runs full 40-step dual-path CFG; whether "no acceleration" means "no step distillation" or "nothing at all" is not clearly defined. Third, Table 1's timing only states resolution, frame count, a single H800, and denoising steps, but does not indicate numerical precision at all; the released code actually loads the transformer in bfloat16 by default and infers under `torch.autocast` (`inference.py`'s `weight_dtype=torch.bfloat16`, `inference_utils.py` loads with `torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16` and wraps the whole forward with `torch.autocast(str(device), dtype=weight_dtype)`), but the paper neither says at which precision the 65 seconds was timed nor states whether the two baselines used the same precision — so the comparison of 65s vs 73s vs 7min20s cannot even verify the basic premise of whether precision is aligned, which is an undocumented condition gap.

### The comparison conditions are uneven and the baselines are too few

There is a condition mismatch in Table 1's quantitative comparison: DiffEraser is evaluated only at $960\times528$ due to its resolution ceiling, while Ours is at 2160×1200; the PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS/VFID of the two are computed on reconstructions at different resolutions and different pixel counts, so directly comparing them in the same table is unfair; the same goes for speed — DiffEraser uses 73 seconds at 960×528, and Ours uses 65 seconds at 2160×1200. Laying out the pixel counts makes the gap more obvious: Ours's $2160\times1200 = 2{,}592{,}000$ pixels is about 5.11× DiffEraser's $960\times528 = 506{,}880$ pixels; given a difference of more than 5× in pixel count, 65 seconds and 73 seconds do not constitute a same-basis throughput comparison, and which is faster is not clear. In addition, the baselines are only the two diffusion methods DiffEraser and MiniMax-Remover; the classic ProPainter and the flow+generation RGVI are both absent from the table.

The authors mark "73s (960*528)" in red in DiffEraser's time column, which amounts to writing the "condition mismatch" directly into the table — Ours is at 2160×1200 while DiffEraser is only at 960×528, so neither the reconstruction metrics nor the time of the two are on the same pixel basis. ### The strongest competitor that was removed happens to be the author of the evaluation dataset

A detail worth being wary of: the HQVI evaluation dataset is exactly the one proposed by RGVI (cho2025elevating), and the paper uses its data but does not put RGVI into the comparison. More critically, in the arXiv source `Formatting-Instructions-LaTeX-2026.tex` there is a commented-out row of RGVI numbers (on HQVI: PSNR 30.10, SSIM 0.9489, LPIPS 0.0357, VFID 0.0058), compared to Ours's 30.78, 0.9446, 0.0343, 0.0072 — RGVI is actually better than EraserDiT on two metrics, SSIM and VFID. That is, the strongest baseline, which is on someone else's dataset and is stronger on some metrics, was removed from the public table. This gives the conclusion "outperforms across all considered evaluation metrics" a clear opponent-picking flavor, and readers should understand it as "relative to these two selected diffusion baselines" rather than universally optimal.

### The flagship contribution CPS has no quantitative ablation, and does not match the released code

CPS is one of the paper's four contributions, but the "Ablation Study on Temporal Consistency" section is entirely textual description ("without CPS there is flickering, and adding it becomes stable"), pushing the evidence to videos in the supplementary/project page, with no quantitative numbers in the main text (e.g. how much VFID drops after removing CPS). Without a quantitative ablation, the improvement cannot be quantitatively attributed to CPS. To make matters worse, as described in First Principles, the released code's long-clip handling is "fixed 9-frame overlap sliding-window streaming," and I could not find the step-by-step cumulative-shift circular implementation of Algorithm 1, nor the CFG-distilled student the paper describes — the publicly verifiable parts do not match the paper's core algorithm description.

### Is it a new method or a repackaging of LTX-Video

Broken down, EraserDiT's backbone, 3D full attention, 3D VAE, rectified flow, and cross-attention prompt injection all come from LTX-Video; concatenating the mask and masked-video latent along the channel as conditioning is essentially an existing inpainting/BrushNet-style conditioning recipe moved onto a DiT. What truly belongs to this paper is (a) the data engine that self-builds about 60,000 dynamic-object mask clips, (b) the idea of CPS, and (c) automatic prompt generation. But (b) degrades in the code into sliding-window overlap, and (c) is a demo script that hardcodes "the woman" and swaps the VLM; the completeness of both is lower than the paper's narrative. This work is more like "engineering LTX-Video into object removal" than an architectural-level innovation.

### Real-world boundaries: high thresholds, with clear failure scenarios

Even accepting its quality claims, the practical boundaries need to be stated clearly: the official README states that 2K video needs >60GB VRAM, effectively restricting single-GPU users to the H800/A100 class; the removed object together with its motion trajectory must lie entirely within a $1088\times1920$ cropping box, otherwise it gives up directly; the paper's Limitations also admits poor results in "fast-flowing water, extremely fast camera motion" scenes. Add that global consistency of long clips has no guarantee (CPS only smooths seams) and that the qualitative comparisons are a carefully hand-picked few cases, and these all remind readers that headline numbers such as 65 seconds and SOTA should be interpreted within the range of "the evaluated inputs and hardware," and not extrapolated.

## One-minute version

- **Task challenge**: large-object video removal must erase large areas of people and subtitles in advertisements or videos, and fill in natural, spatio-temporally coherent new background when there are no historical frames to move. Example: traditional optical-flow and diffusion methods easily generate blur with large masks, e.g. DiffEraser leaves colored patches at tree-trunk edges, while MiniMax-Remover takes more than 7 minutes to process a 2K video.
- **Core architecture**: fine-tune the off-the-shelf text-to-video model LTX-Video, freezing its 3D VAE and text encoder and training only the denoising backbone — this 3D full-attention backbone takes at each step three channel-concatenated features: the noised latent, the masked-video (masked regions zeroed) latent, and the downsampled mask. Example: relying on the $32\times32\times8$ spatio-temporal downsampling to reach about 1:192 compression, a 97-frame video, whose paper non-padded formula corresponds to 13 latent frames (the released streaming implementation first reflect-pads to 121 frames, corresponding to 16 latent frames), is then denoised.
- **Benchmark results**: on DAVIS and HQVI, EraserDiT comprehensively leads on all four quality metrics over these two selected baselines; on speed it is clearly faster than MiniMax-Remover, but the 65s vs 73s against DiffEraser are tabulated numbers at different resolutions (2160×1200 vs 960×528, ~5.11× difference in pixel count), not a same-basis throughput comparison, so which is faster is not clear. Example: on HQVI, EraserDiT's temporal-consistency metric VFID is 0.0072 at 65 seconds, versus MiniMax-Remover's 0.0174 at 7 minutes 20 seconds.
- **Claim flaws**: the paper's flagship 2K high resolution is not the model generating over the whole image, but the code cropping the large video into a 1080p local window, inpainting it, and pasting back; the evaluation also has a suspicion of opponent-picking. Example: if the removed object's motion range exceeds the $1088\times1920$ window, the program (by design) gives up directly; and the source-code comment shows that the competitor RGVI's SSIM (0.9489) and VFID (0.0058) on HQVI are both better than EraserDiT (0.9446, 0.0072), yet it was not listed in the official table.
- **Deployment boundaries**: actual deployment requires top-tier server GPUs, the removed object's motion is restricted, and prompt automation is not truly productized yet. Example: inferring a 2K video needs >60GB VRAM (restricted to the H800/A100 class), the released script hardcodes the prompt to remove "the woman," and quality degrades markedly with fast-flowing water or violent camera motion.
