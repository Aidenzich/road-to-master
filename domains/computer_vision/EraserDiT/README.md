## Introduction

Video inpainting and object removal must fill the masked region with content that "looks real, is semantically correct, and stays temporally stable across frames" — for example, removing a person or subtitles from an advertising clip and restoring a clean background. Historically there have been two main routes. The first is flow-guided propagation, exemplified by ProPainter: it first performs recurrent flow completion to estimate optical flow, then uses dual-domain propagation to move "known pixels that have appeared before" along the flow into the masked frames, and finally uses a mask-guided sparse Transformer to generate the never-seen unknown pixels. The second is video Transformers, such as STTN and FuseFormer, which bring the ViT into the spatio-temporal dimensions. The common weakness of both routes is: when the mask is large, propagation cannot find known pixels to move, and the generative capacity of their generation module (a GAN or a sparse Transformer) is insufficient, leaving obvious blur and mosaic artifacts.

Recent diffusion models have improved generation quality, but each comes at a cost. DiffuEraser uses Stable Diffusion as its inpainting backbone and initializes from ProPainter's output as a prior; however, limited by its video generation capacity, texture naturalness is still insufficient, with common periodic texture artifacts and blur; MiniMax-Remover takes a streamlined two-stage route but inference on a single 2K clip takes over 7 minutes. Meanwhile, text-to-video Diffusion Transformers (DiT) — CogVideoX, HunyuanVideo, LTX-Video — replace separated temporal/spatial attention with 3D full attention and use a 3D VAE to compress jointly along time and space, greatly improving consistency and flicker under large motion. EraserDiT's core claim is precisely this: bring the strong generative power and spatio-temporal consistency of such a video DiT to the object-removal task of "large holes, large motion, high resolution (claimed up to 1080p)."

The authors list four contributions. First, introducing a DiT that uses 3D full attention to maintain the spatio-temporal consistency of high-resolution video during object removal, and relying on the high compression ratio of a 3D Causal VAE to handle up to 1080p. Second, proposing an inference-stage Circular Position-Shift (CPS) strategy to strengthen the temporal consistency of long clips. Third, to train removal of large dynamic objects (people, animals), the authors self-build about 60,000 masked video clips. Fourth, proposing a method that automatically generates a text prompt so the prompt describes the scene "after the target is removed." The paper's most eye-catching efficiency figure is: on a single NVIDIA H800, without using any acceleration method, processing a 97-frame high-resolution video takes only 65 seconds.

How the paper verifies whether the method works is worth stating clearly up front: evaluation uses the DAVIS and HQVI datasets, the quantitative metrics are the four of PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, VFID plus inference time, the baselines compare only the two recent diffusion methods DiffEraser and MiniMax-Remover, and the qualitative comparison and dynamic evidence of temporal consistency are placed in the videos on the project page. The mask sources of the two datasets are not the same: on DAVIS the authors generate binary masks themselves and reconstruct the masked video accordingly, while on HQVI they directly use the ground-truth masks provided by the dataset — this difference is worth keeping in mind when interpreting cross-dataset numbers (especially for DAVIS, where the masks are self-generated). Overall this is a concise technical report presented in engineering-report form; the main text has no user study and no quantitative ablation of the flagship contribution CPS (see Critical Assessment).

## First Principles

### Backbone choice: why LTX-Video

EraserDiT is not a DiT trained from scratch; it takes Lightricks' LTX-Video as the base architecture and fine-tunes it. The authors' trade-off is direct: HunyuanVideo has the best generation quality but at 720p, 129 frames it needs 60GB of VRAM and inference is slow; LTX-Video achieves about a 1:192 compression ratio via $32\times32\times8$ spatio-temporal downsampling, buying extremely fast inference — although its pure text-to-video quality is not as good as HunyuanVideo, video inpainting is a task "with strong references" (most pixels come from the original clip) and thus demands less generative power, making the speed-first LTX-Video a worthwhile choice. In the whole pipeline, the 3D VAE and text encoder (T5) are frozen, and only all parameters of the denoising video transformer (DiT) are fine-tuned; the three submodules loaded by the official `inference_utils.py` — `AutoencoderKLLTXVideo`, `T5EncoderModel`, `LTXVideoTransformer3DModel` — and the `FlowMatchEulerDiscreteScheduler` correspond exactly to this design.

![Figure 2 (paper Figure 2, training pipeline): the prompt passes through the text encoder (frozen), the masked video passes through the 3D VAE Enc (frozen) to obtain a latent, which is concatenated along the channel dimension (C) with the mask sequence and noise and fed into DiT×n (RMSNorm→Self-attention→Cross-attention→RMSNorm→FFN, only this block is trainable, marked with a flame in the figure); text is injected via cross-attention, and finally the 3D VAE Dec (frozen) decodes the object-removed video.](imgs/train_pipeline.png)

### How the input is constructed and how conditions are fed

At training time, a background video $V_i$ and a mask video $M_j$ are randomly sampled, and the paper writes the pixel-wise multiplication $V_M = V_i \ast M_j$ to obtain the masked video, which is then compressed by the 3D VAE encoder at $32\times32\times8$ into a latent; the corresponding mask sequence is also downsampled to the same scale. There is a detail the paper leaves blank that can only be filled in by cross-referencing the code: the main text never defines the polarity of $M_j$ (whether the removed region is labeled 0 or 1) and only gives the expression $V_i \ast M_j$ — if, taken literally, the multiplication is to zero out the removed region, then $M_j$ would have to be a mask that is "1 for background, 0 for target." But the released code uses the opposite polarity: `pre.py` first thresholds the target mask to 1 (`torch.where(mask_align>(255/2*self.threshold),1,0)`, the removed target region is 1), then computes the masked video as `video*(1-mask)` (the source comment reads `# mask to be white`) — that is, it uses $1-M$ to flip back the "target is 1" mask. The end effect is the same (the removed region is zeroed), but the paper's $V_i \ast M_j$ notation is left blank on polarity, and the actual mask convention has to be read from the code. The input to the denoising transformer is formed by concatenating along the channel dimension the "noised latent," the "masked-video latent," and the "downsampled mask"; the prompt, after being encoded by T5, is fused with the video tokens via cross-attention. The official `pipeline_ltx_video2video.py` confirms this conditioning structure: at each step the transformer simultaneously takes `hidden_states` (noisy latent), `cond_latents` (masked-video latent) and `mask_values` (mask), and `num_channels_latents = (in_channels - 1) // 2` corresponds exactly to the "two latents + one mask" channel layout. Notably, the official inference is in fact video-to-video: it noises the masked-video latent with `strength=0.8` as the starting point, rather than generating from pure Gaussian noise.

Because the 3D Causal VAE also does 8× compression along the temporal dimension, $n$ pixel frames are compressed into a latent temporal length of

$$
f_{\text{lat}} = 1 + \frac{n-1}{8}
$$

that is, "the first frame is independent, and every subsequent 8 frames are merged into 1 latent frame." This brings a mask-alignment detail: every 8 consecutive mask frames must be merged into 1 mask. The official `pre.py`'s `mask_video_nchw` takes the "union" over these 8 frames (`torch.sum(...) >= 1 → 255`, marked as masked if any single frame is masked), which is the correct approach; the paper's main text, however, writes "intersected," a wording inconsistent with the code — an intersection would miss masks that appear in only some frames. The code additionally first applies 9 iterations of cross-kernel dilation to the mask (`dilate_iter=9`, `ksize=(9,9)`) before compressing. Taking the paper's flagship test of 97 frames as an example, the formula without frame padding gives $1+(97-1)/8 = 13$ latent frames, and the 81 frames used in training compress to 11; but note that this 13 is only the paper-level formula value — the released inference code first reflect-pads the clip to `TEMP_INFER_LEN=121` before feeding the DiT, so the actual latent temporal length is 16 rather than 13 (derivation in the later section "One concrete forward pass").

### Inference flow and automatic prompting

At inference time, the user only needs to draw a bounding box around the object to be removed on the first frame; the role of this box is to condition a VLM (the paper says "e.g. Qwen2.5-VL") to automatically generate a prompt that "describes the scene after the target is removed." Note that the mask sequence $M_{test}$ is obtained in a separate step: the paper's original text only writes "based on the prompt … a mask sequence $M_{test}$ can be obtained," and never explains the actual mask-generation method — that is, "producing the mask directly from the box" is not written in the paper, and this step is left blank in the main text. After obtaining the mask, it is pixel-wise multiplied with the original clip to get the masked video, and the three are fed together into EraserDiT to get the output. And in the released code this gap is even more evident: `inference.py` does not automatically produce a mask at all — a mask video must be supplied externally by the user; the argument `--mask_path` (default `data/10268234_mask.mp4`) is a required input, and `run_batch` passes that video into preprocessing as `mask_path=video_mask_path`; conversely `--bbox_path` defaults to `None` and is only used when the resolution exceeds about 1080p and a ROI needs to be cropped. As for "automatic prompt generation," it is also not yet truly productized: the released `img_caption.py` in fact uses MiniCPM-o-2.6 (not the Qwen2.5-VL the paper mentions), and it is a standalone demo script carrying the author's local absolute path and a hard-coded "Remove the woman," not wired into `inference.py` (whose `--prompt` must be provided manually by the user, with the default value "There is a bridge over the lake.").

### Circular Position-Shift: paper description vs released implementation

CPS targets the scenario where "the inference clip length exceeds the maximum length seen in training." The paper's approach (Algorithm 1, Figure 4) is: first, in reflect mode, reflect-pad the length-$l$ sequence $[0,l]$ with $[l-1,1]$, forming a circular sequence with cycle length

$$
L = 2l - 2
$$

On the circular sequence, any contiguous subsequence is a physically coherent piece of video. Then a cumulative shift $\alpha_\sigma$ is set, adding $\alpha$ at each denoising timestep, so that the length-$f$ sliding window cuts into the sequence from a different position at different timesteps, thereby repeatedly "smoothing out" the seams at clip boundaries. Because sliding-window inference nearly doubles the cost, the authors additionally use CFG distillation to distill the "unconditional + conditional" two-path outputs into a single student, trained at a fixed guidance scale of $3.0$, bringing the cost back to the original level.

![Figure 4 (paper Figure 4, Circular Position-Shift): the top row is the original sequence frame 0→n; the middle row, after reflect padding, appends the reversed sequence frame n-1→1, forming a cycle (the purple arrow indicates head-to-tail connection); the green, yellow, black, and red boxes in the bottom row are sliding windows cutting in at different positions, and the rightmost red dashed box crosses the tail of the cycle and returns to the beginning, illustrating "circular" and "shift."](imgs/cycle.png)

However, after static inspection of the released code, I could not find, in the single-GPU inference path, the "cumulative $\alpha_\sigma$ per timestep, sliding window" implementation of Algorithm 1. The long-clip mechanism I could locate is a different, more naive streaming: `inference.py` processes batch by batch in units of `TEMP_INFER_LEN=121`, retaining `shift_alpha = 1*8+1 = 9` overlapping frames between batches (`pre_video_shift = output_frames[-9:]` connected to the start of the next batch), and when the last batch is short it is padded with `pre.py`'s `flip(0)[1:-1]` (reflect); each batch itself is a vid2vid denoising with one full attention over the 121 pixel frames — note that `TEMP_INFER_LEN=121` is the pixel-frame batch length of preprocessing, not the latent length; by the $1+(n-1)/8$ rule, 121 pixel frames correspond to $1+(121-1)/8 = 16$ temporal latent frames. Moreover, the released denoising loop actually runs full dual-path CFG (two transformer forwards per step, `guidance_scale=3`), rather than the "single CFG-distilled student" the paper describes for offsetting the doubled cost. Therefore CPS, this flagship contribution, appears in the public code as "sliding-window streaming with a fixed 9-frame overlap + tail reflect padding," which is formally not the same as the step-by-step circular shift of the paper's Algorithm 1 — this is a gap in reproducibility.

### High resolution is actually achieved by "cropping"

The paper's title and abstract tout 2K / high resolution, but the implementation reveals a key premise: in `inference.py`, `crop_flag = True if height*width > 1088*1920 else False`, so once a video exceeds about 2K, cropping is triggered. `pre.py`'s `bbox_cal` does not read any mask frame or mask video, but opens the CSV pointed to by `--bbox_path` (whose argument help string is mislabeled "Input mask path"), parses its `bboxes` column row by row, takes the `inference_idx`-th bounding box of each row, and after collecting them computes the four coordinate extremes (`x1_min`, `x2_max`, `y1_min`, `y2_max`), then centers on these extremes to crop a $1920\times1088$ (or $1088\times1920$ for portrait) window; by the code logic, if the horizontal total span of these boxes exceeds the window (`x2_max - x1_min > crop_w`, or likewise for height), `bbox_cal` returns `None`, and the main program outputs "The maximum mask movement in the current video sequence exceeds 1080 × 1920" and gives up. But this is "design intent" rather than actual execution behavior I can verify from the released code: `inference.py:52` calls `preprocessor.bbox_video(height, width, bbox_path)` with only 3 arguments, but `pre.py:75`'s `bbox_video` also requires a 4th parameter `inference_idx`; as released, the high-resolution (cropping) branch would first throw a `TypeError` on this call and never reach the movement check above. So what is described here is a call incompatibility found in the static code, and readers should not treat "graceful giving up" as a verified execution result. In any case, by design the DiT never does full attention over the complete 2160×2100; instead it first crops to a $\le 1088\times1920$ (essentially ≤1080p) window around the object, generates on that window, and pastes it back into the original image. This explains the seeming contradiction of "the contribution says up to 1080p, but the abstract says 2160×2100": the model's generation-resolution ceiling is 1080p, and higher resolution is merely cropped-then-processed. The cost is a restricted use scenario — the removed object, together with its motion trajectory, must lie entirely within one $1088\times1920$ box.

### Training, data, and efficiency settings (overview)

The table below organizes the settings and cost figures from the paper and code; note that these are "settings and costs," not evaluation results.

| Aspect | Setting | Specific numbers / source |
|-|-|-|
| Backbone | LTX-Video fine-tuned, only the video transformer is trained | 3D VAE, T5 frozen |
| Training resolution / frames | fixed resolution, fixed frame count | $960\times960$, 81 frames |
| Iterations / hardware | Adam-W, Rectified Flow | 280k iters, 24× A100(80G), lr $3\times10^{-5}$ |
| Loss (two-stage) | first 150k use L2; then switch to Focal Area | see formula below |
| frame sampling step | random sampling step at training to strengthen large motion | 1–6 |
| Background video | diverse backgrounds downloaded from Pexels | 600,000 clips |
| mask data | SA-V + self-built animal/person masks (extracted by SAM 2 / Grounded SAM 2) | animals ~30,000 (1–5s) + people ~32,000 (5–20s) ≈ 60,000 |
| Inference steps | official `num_inference_steps=50` × `strength=0.8` | effective 40 steps |
| Inference clip length | `TEMP_INFER_LEN` | 121 frames (overlap 9) |
| Flagship efficiency | 2160×1200, 97 frames, H800, no acceleration | 65 seconds (needs >60GB VRAM) |

The second-stage Focal Area loss focuses the loss on the mask region:

$$
L_{\text{focal}} = L_2 \cdot (1 + D_{\text{mask}})
$$

where $D_{\text{mask}}$ is the dilated mask of $M_j^{input}$, equivalent to weighting inside the mask (where content is to be generated), making the model work harder to learn reconstruction of the removed region.

### One concrete forward pass: how the flagship efficiency test runs

Taking the efficiency test of the paper's Table 1 as an example, let's walk through its "by-design" data flow (as the previous section noted, the released code's cropping branch throws a `TypeError` first due to argument incompatibility, so the following describes design intent rather than a directly runnable path): input a 2160×1200, 97-frame video. Because $2160\times1200 > 1088\times1920$, cropping is triggered, and by the extremes of the per-row `bboxes` coordinates in the `bbox_path` CSV (rather than any mask frame) a $1920\times1088$ window is cropped; after aligning to a multiple of 32, spatial downsampling by 32× gives about a $60\times34$ latent grid. The temporal dimension must be viewed in two layers: the paper-level formula without frame padding gives $1+(97-1)/8 = 13$ latent frames, but the released code's streaming preprocessing does not feed 97 frames directly — `pre.py`'s first-batch path (`batch_idx==0`), by `TEMP_INFER_LEN=121` and `shift_alpha=9`, computes that $121-97 = 24$ more frames are still needed, and reflect-pads (`flip(0)[1:-1]`) to 121 frames; `inference_utils.py` then calls the pipeline with `num_frames=videos.shape[0]=121`, and `pipeline_ltx_video2video.py` obtains 16 temporal latent frames by $(121-1)//8+1 = 16$. So on the released path, the latent the DiT actually processes is about $[1, C, 16, 34, 60]$ (the temporal dimension is 16, from the padded 121, not the 13 of the unpadded formula). On the conditioning side, after concatenating the masked-video latent and the downsampled mask along the channel dimension, it noises from `strength=0.8` and runs 40 steps of rectified-flow denoising, each step doing dual-path CFG with `guidance_scale=3`; after decoding back to pixels it is pasted back into the original 2160×1200 frame. The whole thing takes about 65 seconds on a single H800, compared with MiniMax-Remover taking 7 min 20 s for the same input with 6 steps — EraserDiT's speed advantage mainly comes from LTX-Video's high-compression backbone, not fewer denoising steps.

## 🧪 Critical Assessment

### The problem is real, but this is an engineering-flavored technical report

Video removal of subtitles, logos, passersby, and animals is a real and high-frequency need in media and short-video production lines (the authors are from Mango TV, a reasonable background). The method makes pragmatic trade-offs in backbone selection, data engineering, and inference engineering. But it must be emphasized: this is a concise technical report, and the main text lacks quantitative verification of the flagship contribution and rigorous controlled design — reviewed point by point below.

### The flagship numbers are self-contradictory, and the "no acceleration" claim is questionable

The same efficiency selling point does not line up within the paper itself: the abstract and the official README both write 2160×2100, 97 frames, 65 seconds, but Table 1's caption and main text write 2160×1200, 97 frames, 65 seconds (an earlier version of the caption in the source even says 2160×1200, 121 frames). The flagship resolution number is inconsistent between the abstract and the table, and readers cannot tell which is the actual measured condition. Second, the abstract claims "without any acceleration method," but the paper itself describes CFG distillation, a cost-reduction technique, while the released code runs full dual-path CFG at 40 steps; whether "no acceleration" means "no step distillation was used" or "nothing at all was used" is not clearly defined. Third, Table 1's timing only states resolution, frame count, a single H800, and denoising steps, but completely omits numerical precision; the released code in fact loads the transformer in bfloat16 by default and infers under `torch.autocast` (`inference.py`'s `weight_dtype=torch.bfloat16`, `inference_utils.py` loads with `torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16` and wraps the whole forward with `torch.autocast(str(device), dtype=weight_dtype)`), but the paper neither says under which precision the 65 seconds is timed nor states whether the two baselines use the same precision — so the comparison of 65s vs 73s vs 7min20s cannot even verify the basic premise of whether precision is aligned, an undocumented condition gap.

### Unmatched comparison conditions, too few baselines

Table 1's quantitative comparison has a condition mismatch: DiffEraser is evaluated only at $960\times528$ due to its resolution ceiling, while our method is at 2160×1200, so their PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS/VFID are computed on reconstructions at different resolutions and different pixel counts, and comparing them directly in the same table is not fair; speed is the same — DiffEraser takes 73 seconds at 960×528, our method 65 seconds at 2160×1200. Laying out the pixel counts makes the gap more obvious: our method's $2160\times1200 = 2{,}592{,}000$ pixels is about 5.11× DiffEraser's $960\times528 = 506{,}880$ pixels; with a pixel-count difference of more than 5×, 65 seconds versus 73 seconds does not constitute a same-basis throughput comparison, and which is faster is unclear. Moreover, the baselines are only the two diffusion methods DiffEraser and MiniMax-Remover; the classic ProPainter and the flow-plus-generation RGVI are both absent from the table.

### The strongest rival that was removed is exactly the author of the evaluation dataset

A detail worth being wary of: the HQVI evaluation dataset is precisely the one proposed by RGVI (cho2025elevating), yet the paper uses its data without putting RGVI into the comparison. More critically, the arXiv source `Formatting-Instructions-LaTeX-2026.tex` contains a commented-out row of RGVI data (on HQVI: PSNR 30.10, SSIM 0.9489, LPIPS 0.0357, VFID 0.0058), compared with our method's 30.78, 0.9446, 0.0343, 0.0072 — RGVI is in fact better than EraserDiT on both SSIM and VFID. That is, the strongest baseline, on someone else's dataset and stronger on some metrics, was removed from the public table. This gives the conclusion "outperforms across all considered evaluation metrics" a clear flavor of opponent-picking, and readers should understand it as "relative to these two selected diffusion baselines" rather than universally best.

### The flagship contribution CPS has no quantitative ablation and does not match the released code

CPS is one of the paper's four contributions, but the "Ablation Study on Temporal Consistency" section is entirely prose ("without CPS there is flickering, with it added it is stable"), pushing the evidence to the videos on the supplementary/project page, with no quantitative numbers in the main text (e.g. how much VFID drops after removing CPS). Without a quantitative ablation, the quantified improvement cannot be attributed to CPS. To make matters worse, as described in First Principles, the released code's long-clip processing is "sliding-window streaming with a fixed 9-frame overlap"; I could not find the circular implementation of Algorithm 1's step-by-step cumulative shift, nor the CFG-distilled student the paper describes — the publicly verifiable part does not match the paper's core algorithm description.

### A new method, or repackaging LTX-Video

Taken apart, EraserDiT's backbone, 3D full attention, 3D VAE, rectified flow, and cross-attention prompt injection all come from LTX-Video; concatenating the mask and masked-video latent along the channel as conditioning is essentially the existing inpainting / BrushNet-style conditioning recipe moved onto a DiT. What genuinely belongs to this paper is (a) the data engine that self-builds about 60,000 dynamic-object mask clips, (b) the CPS idea, and (c) automatic prompt generation. But (b) degrades to sliding-window overlap in the code, and (c) is a demo script with a hard-coded "the woman" and a swapped VLM — both are less complete than the paper describes. This work is more like "engineering LTX-Video into object removal" than an architecture-level innovation.

### Real-world boundary: high threshold, with clear failure scenarios

Even accepting its quality claims, the practical boundary must be stated clearly: the official README states that 2K video needs >60GB VRAM, effectively limiting single-GPU users to the H800/A100 class; the removed object, together with its motion trajectory, must lie entirely within a $1088\times1920$ cropping box, otherwise it gives up outright; the paper's Limitations also admits poor results in "fast water flow, extremely fast camera motion" scenarios. Adding that long-clip global consistency has no guarantee (CPS only smooths seams) and that the qualitative comparison is a carefully picked few cases, these all remind readers that headline numbers like 65 seconds and SOTA should be interpreted within the range of "the evaluated inputs and hardware" and not extrapolated.

## One-minute version

- **Task problem**: large-object video removal must erase large areas of people and subtitles from ads or videos and fill in natural, spatio-temporally coherent new background when there are no history frames to move. Example: traditional optical-flow and diffusion methods tend to generate blur under large masks, e.g. DiffEraser leaves colored patches at tree-trunk edges, while MiniMax-Remover takes over 7 minutes to process a 2K clip.
- **Core architecture**: fine-tune the off-the-shelf text-to-video model LTX-Video, freeze its 3D VAE and text encoder, and train only the denoising backbone — this 3D full-attention backbone takes in at each step three features concatenated along the channel: the noised latent, the latent of the masked video (after the masked region is zeroed), and the downsampled mask. Example: relying on $32\times32\times8$ spatio-temporal downsampling to reach about a 1:192 compression ratio, a 97-frame video — whose paper unpadded formula corresponds to 13 latent frames (the released streaming implementation first reflect-pads to 121 frames, corresponding to 16 latent frames) — is then denoised.
- **Benchmark results**: on DAVIS and HQVI, EraserDiT leads across all four quality metrics against these two selected baselines; on speed it is clearly faster than MiniMax-Remover, but the 65 seconds vs 73 seconds against DiffEraser are tabulated numbers at different resolutions (2160×1200 vs 960×528, a pixel-count difference of about 5.11×), not a same-basis throughput comparison, and which is faster is unclear. Example: on HQVI, EraserDiT's temporal-consistency metric VFID is 0.0072 and it takes 65 seconds, versus MiniMax-Remover's 0.0174 and 7 min 20 s.
- **Claim flaws**: the paper's flagship 2K high resolution is not the model generating over the whole image, but the code cropping the large video to a 1080p local window, inpainting, and pasting it back; the evaluation also has a whiff of opponent-picking. Example: if the removed object's motion range exceeds the $1088\times1920$ window, the program (by design) gives up outright; and source comments show the rival RGVI beats EraserDiT on HQVI in both SSIM (0.9489) and VFID (0.0058) over EraserDiT's (0.9446, 0.0072), yet it was not listed in the official table.
- **Deployment boundary**: actual deployment needs top-tier server GPUs, the removed object's motion is restricted, and prompt automation is not yet truly productized. Example: inferring a 2K video needs >60GB VRAM (limited to the H800/A100 class), the released script hard-codes the prompt to remove "the woman," and quality degrades noticeably under fast water flow or violent camera motion.
