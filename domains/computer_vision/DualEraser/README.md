## Introduction

Ordinary video object removal only paints out the object designated by the mask, yet leaves behind the physical effects it caused—for example, the tire smoke kicked up by a racing car, the emitted light a desk lamp casts on a wall, the wake and ripple on a water surface, the reflection in a mirror. Keeping these "causes" while erasing the "effects" produces physically inconsistent frames (e.g., the car is gone but the smoke remains). What DualEraser aims to solve is **joint video object and effect removal**: erasing the object and its associated effects at the same time, while disturbing the unrelated background as little as possible.

The authors attribute the difficulty of doing this well to a fundamental conflict they call the **semantic–pixel conflict**, which they split into two levels. The first level is the condition-level modality dissonance: information from a single modality is incomplete (a binary mask carries almost no semantics for a weakly spatiotemporally correlated effect like "spreading smoke"), while the relative dominance of the two conditions—text and mask—drifts with the scene. The second level is the optimization-level objective entanglement: semantic erasure (dare to delete) and pixel fidelity (don't move things around) are two objectives crammed into the same set of network weights, where they pull against each other.

DualEraser's high-level solution maps to three modules: use **Bipartite Text** to explicitly write out "what to remove + what the scene should look like afterward," paired with **MCCE**'s conditional dropout training, to fill back the semantics the mask lacks; use **LD-CFG**, a learnable fusion module, to replace fixed-scale classifier-free guidance so that the text/mask weighting automatically balances with the scene; and use the decoupled architecture of **Locator (high-noise expert) + Preserver (low-noise expert)** to hand "erasure" and "fidelity" to two experts with different training recipes.

For evaluation, quantitative results use two benchmarks that provide ground-truth: the ROSE Benchmark and VOR-Eval, with metrics PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS; the open world (no GT) uses VOR-Wild, with metrics Erasure Preference Rate (EPR) and User Preference Rate (UPR), two human preference rates. Baselines fall into two categories: object-removal-only ProPainter, MiniMax-Remover, DiffuEraser; and joint object+effect removal ROSE, EffectErase, SVOR, Generative Omnimatte. The headline numbers are that the 5B version beats SVOR by 2.16 dB PSNR on ROSE and beats EffectErase by 1.44 dB on VOR-Eval.

## First Principles

### Two-level conflict: the paper's problem framing

The first symptom of the first-level condition-level issue is "single-modality information is incomplete": a pixel-level binary mask only outlines the pixels the object occupies, and has no semantic description for effects like smoke or emitted light that are weakly correlated with the object and spread outside the mask, so the model can only "guess" which pixels belong to the effect. The second symptom is "cross-modal dominance imbalance": as Figure 2 shows, the water-surface small-boat row needs text to dominate (the mask only frames the boat and cannot erase the wake behind its stern), whereas the stroller row needs the mask to dominate (to preserve the other child and the person behind, who are in the frame but not masked); the relative importance of the two modalities can flip entirely.

The second-level optimization-level objective entanglement has direct evidence. The authors observe a single expert at early vs. late training: Table 1 shows that late training raises ROSE PSNR from 30.39 to 32.86 and VOR-Eval from 23.29 to 23.53—pixel reconstruction improves—but the open-world EPR instead drops from 0.4987 to 0.4346. That is, the longer it trains, the more it overfits to "copying the unedited region back verbatim," and instead loses the ability, on real video, to "dare to erase the effect cleanly." With these two objectives stuffed into the same set of weights, a single model struggles to excel at both.

### Data flow: from input to output

Given a reference video $V_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and mask $M$, the masked video is defined as $V_{\mathrm{m}} = V_{\mathrm{ref}} \odot (\mathbf{1}-M)$. The three visual input streams are each encoded into latents: $x_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and the masked-video latent $x_{\mathrm{m}}$ are obtained by a frozen VAE encoder, while the mask latent $m$ is just the mask **resized** to the latent scale (as in the left half of Figure 4, the mask takes the Resize path rather than the VAE). The Bipartite Text prompt $\mathcal{P}$ is encoded into $c_{\mathrm{txt}}$ by a frozen text encoder. The backbone uses the flow-matching MMDiT of Wan2.2 5B (default) and Wan2.1 1.3B, and the training objective is to predict the velocity field:

$$
x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,\epsilon,\qquad v_t^{\star} = \frac{\partial x_t}{\partial t} = \epsilon - x_0
$$

where $x_0$ is the latent of the target (already-removed) video $V_{\mathrm{gt}}$, $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$, and $t\in[0,1]$ is the flow-matching timestep. At inference, starting from noise, the Locator first takes the high-noise steps, then hands off to the Preserver for the low-noise steps; after running 40 steps it takes the clean latent $x^{\mathrm{f}}_0$ of the full-guidance branch and restores it to the output video via the VAE decoder. It is worth pointing out that the paper nowhere gives the supported resolution, frame count / video length, or fps—these input boundaries are undocumented, and this note does not speculate about them. Only two preprocessing steps are explicitly recorded: during training, an affine transformation is applied to the video to increase camera-viewpoint diversity, and erosion and dilation are applied to the input mask to improve the model's robustness to imprecise masks. As for the mask source, the appendix explains that the masks for the qualitative evaluation samples are mostly auto-generated by SAM3 and explicitly states they are "not always precise" (e.g., the mirror example has boundary errors, the hand example frames only part of the arm), meaning that in practice mask quality is subject to automatic-segmentation error, and DualEraser must rely on the semantics of the Bipartite Text to fill in the target beyond the mask boundary.

![Figure 4 (MyMethod.pdf): the overall DualEraser framework. Left is Stage I (MCCE): Target/Reference/Mask/Masked video are each turned into latents by a frozen VAE encoder or Resize, noised, and then—via Random Conditional Dropout—produce three kinds of guidance (text-only / mask-only / full), training the Locator/Preserver (trainable DiT) and outputting three velocity streams. Right is inference: the three conditioning streams first pass through the Locator (high noise) then the Preserver (low noise), taking the full branch and restoring it via the VAE decoder.](imgs/framework.png)

### MCCE and Bipartite Text: filling the missing semantics back in

Bipartite Text is not an ordinary caption but an explicit two-part structure: the removal semantics writes out "the object to remove + its associated effects," and the reconstruction semantics writes out "what the scene should look like after removal." These prompts are auto-generated by Qwen3-VL 8B in both training and evaluation, taking as input the reference video plus a "target-object-only video" that keeps only the masked target region and removes the background, so the VLM can both identify the object and infer its effects.

MCCE (Multi-Conditional Capability Elicitation) is the Stage I training strategy: on top of MMDiT's original CFG that "randomly drops the text condition," it additionally, with some probability, zeros out the mask latent $m$ and the masked-video latent $x_{\mathrm{m}}$ together, so the model is forced to operate under all three conditioning configurations:

$$
c^{\mathrm{txt}}_t = (x_t,t,x_{\mathrm{ref}},\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0},c_{\mathrm{txt}}),\quad
c^{\mathrm{m}}_t = (x_t,t,x_{\mathrm{ref}},m,x_{\mathrm{m}},\varnothing),\quad
c^{\mathrm{f}}_t = (x_t,t,x_{\mathrm{ref}},m,x_{\mathrm{m}},c_{\mathrm{txt}})
$$

In implementation, the text-condition dropout probability is set to 0.1, and the probability of zeroing the mask and masked-video together is set to 0.2; the "all-unconditional" cell is deliberately excluded, because its randomness conflicts with the deterministic control that removal requires. The text-only cell forces the model to reconstruct the target from text alone, strengthening text-to-visual alignment. The ablation (Table 3) shows: $p=0$ is equivalent to the Wan2.2 baseline, and under most training lengths $p>0$ yields better EPR than $p=0$ (e.g., at 2000 steps $p=0.2$'s 0.4000 vs. $p=0$'s 0.3692), supporting the effect of this mask dropout.

### From MC-CFG to LD-CFG: making guidance learnable

As a contrast, the authors first extend traditional CFG into an MC-CFG baseline that can consume both text and mask, using two manual scales $\omega_{\mathrm{m}}$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{txt}}$ to sequentially extrapolate, rescale, and then fuse:

$$
\tilde{v}_t = v^{\mathrm{m}}_t + \omega_{\mathrm{m}}(v^{\mathrm{f}}_t - v^{\mathrm{m}}_t),\quad
\hat{v}_t = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{\lVert v^{\mathrm{f}}_t\rVert_2}{\lVert\tilde{v}_t\rVert_2+\delta},0,1\right)\tilde{v}_t,\quad
v_t = v^{\mathrm{txt}}_t + \omega_{\mathrm{txt}}(\hat{v}_t - v^{\mathrm{txt}}_t)
$$

The problem is that this set of scales is globally fixed and cannot be adjusted per scene; moreover, MC-CFG is very sensitive to scale (the appendix shows the ROSE-Bench optimum falls at text=1.0/mask=3.0, whereas VOR-Eval has both at 1.5—switch the benchmark and the optimum moves). LD-CFG's approach replaces "tuning scale" with "learning fusion": Stage II switches to deterministic inputs running text-only / mask-only / joint in parallel, and after each DiT block attaches two learnable linear projections $f^{\mathrm{m}}_i$ and $f^{\mathrm{txt}}_i$ that residually fuse the three feature streams:

$$
\hat{h}^{\mathrm{f}}_{i+1} = \tilde{h}^{\mathrm{f}}_{i+1} + f^{\mathrm{m}}_i(\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{f}}_{i+1} - h^{\mathrm{m}}_{i+1}),\qquad
h^{\mathrm{f}}_{i+1} = \hat{h}^{\mathrm{f}}_{i+1} + f^{\mathrm{txt}}_i(\hat{h}^{\mathrm{f}}_{i+1} - h^{\mathrm{txt}}_{i})
$$

The DiT block itself is frozen, and only these projections are trainable; at inference the three streams are batch-wise concatenated into the network, and only the full branch $v^{\mathrm{f}}_t$ is taken forward to the next denoise step ($dx_t = v^{\mathrm{f}}_t\,dt$). LD-CFG therefore internalizes "who dominates, text or mask" into the network weights rather than a scalar at inference time. The ablation (Table 4) shows it wins across all three benchmarks: ROSE 33.33 / VOR-Eval 23.91 / VOR-Wild EPR 0.5846, beating Standard MC-CFG (32.91 / 23.79 / 0.5154); and it beats Simple Linear (32.16 / 23.41 / 0.5077), which has the same parameter count but just hard-wires a linear layer, showing the gain comes from the fusion design rather than the extra parameters.

### Locator–Preserver: splitting the two objectives between two experts

To break objective entanglement, the authors split the denoising trajectory by noise level into two experts: the Locator is the high-noise expert, responsible for identifying and erasing the object and its effects; the Preserver is the low-noise expert, responsible for faithfully preserving the background. Three key differences make them specialize in different objectives: (1) training length—the Locator has fewer steps (12,500 steps for 5B) to preserve high-level semantic erasure, and the Preserver has more steps (20,000 steps) to polish pixel alignment; (2) training data—the Locator uses diverse synthetic + real mixed data, and the Preserver uses only "pixel-clean" data; (3) the objective itself—one must "dare to delete," the other must "not move things." The authors deliberately point out that this differs from Wan2.2's built-in noise-level dual-expert (high noise does layout, low noise does detail) along all three dimensions of objective, length, and data, and so is not merely reused.

Why does the Preserver not use real data? Because real pairs (such as VOR) often have background-misalignment problems: the appendix Table 7 measures VOR (Real) with a background MAE as high as 11.50 at 1/16 crop, while ROSE (Synthetic) is only 3.85. If the Preserver were trained on misaligned real pairs, it would fit these artifacts. The ablation (Table 6) confirms this: training the Preserver with ROSE only, even for just 5,000 steps, yields a higher reference-background PSNR (53.60) than training on VOR–ROSE mixed data for 40,000 steps (52.97). The overall training data is mixed from VOR (about 60K pairs) and ROSE (about 17K pairs, after oversampling its four non-common effect categories) into about 100K sampled pairs; Stage I 5B totals 32.5K expert-steps, 1.3B totals 35K, and Stage II each expert takes another 1,800 steps (3.6K total). The noise-level boundary (routing boundary) is set to 0.875, the default adopted after comparing {0.675, 0.775, 0.875} in the appendix—it has the highest VOR-Wild EPR at three of the four Locator training durations (2,000 / 3,000 / 4,000 steps), and only at 1,000 steps does 0.675 (0.3897) exceed 0.875 (0.3692). Throughout, 16 A100s, batch size 2, 40 inference steps.

### One concrete forward pass: the water-surface boat as example

Take a walk through the top-row text-dominant scene of Figure 2: the input is a top-down reference video of a boat on the water; the mask (yellow outline) frames only the boat body; the Bipartite Text's removal section writes "remove the boat and its shadow / wake / ripple," and the reconstruction section writes "restore to a calm, undisturbed green water surface." Encoding yields $x_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (VAE), $x_{\mathrm{m}}$ (the VAE latent of the masked video, with the boat's location hollowed out), $m$ (the resized mask), and $c_{\mathrm{txt}}$ (text encoder). After the three conditioning tuples $c^{\mathrm{txt}}_t, c^{\mathrm{m}}_t, c^{\mathrm{f}}_t$ are batch-concatenated, the Locator first decides at the high-noise steps (the side with noise level above 0.875) that "both the boat and the wake should disappear," then the Preserver fills the surrounding water back with continuous texture at the low-noise steps; after each block, LD-CFG fuses the three streams with $f^{\mathrm{m}}_i, f^{\mathrm{txt}}_i$. Here, if only the mask were given (mask-only), because the wake falls outside the mask, the model would erase the boat but leave the wake—this is exactly why text needs to dominate. After running 40 steps, taking the full branch, and VAE-decoding, we get the removal result. The appendix's mask ablation gives the reverse numbers for the same mechanism: removing the mask condition entirely and keeping only the Bipartite Text drops ROSE-Bench PSNR from 33.55 to 30.32 (−3.23 dB) and VOR-Eval from 23.87 to 21.30 (−2.57 dB), showing that text supplies semantics and mask supplies pixel localization, and neither can be missing.

The table below is the main quantitative comparison (excerpted from Table 2, ↑ higher is better, ↓ lower is better):

|-|-|-|-|-|-|
| EffectErase | 27.04 | 0.0679 | 22.47 | 0.4256 | 0.4462 |
| ROSE | 31.09 | 0.0527 | 22.07 | 0.3744 | 0.3744 |

The 5B version beats the strongest baseline SVOR (31.17) by 2.16 dB on ROSE and beats EffectErase (22.47) by 1.44 dB on VOR-Eval, and takes both the highest EPR and UPR on VOR-Wild; even the 1.3B version beats all baselines. Qualitatively (Figure 1, the cover teaser) DualEraser is cleaner than its competitors on all six effect categories—smoke, deformation, light, mirror, shadow, reflection: competitors commonly show background breakage (red box) or fail to erase cleanly (blue box), e.g., Gen. In addition, the prompt-completeness ablation shows that the full Bipartite Text's VOR-Wild EPR (0.5923) is higher than any incomplete setting (empty 0.5231, object-only 0.5282, effect-only 0.5256, background-only 0.5385), but the pixel metrics barely move—implying that text mainly helps open-world semantic erasure rather than benchmark pixel scores.

## 🧪 Critical Assessment

### Is the semantic–pixel conflict a real problem

The problem is real: erasing an object while leaving its shadow, smoke, or reflection does produce physical inconsistency, which is a practical pain point in film post-production and content creation. The authors' two-level decomposition of the "semantic–pixel conflict" (modality dissonance / objective entanglement) is not empty talk either; Table 1 and Figure 3 provide direct evidence of "the longer it trains, the better the pixels and the worse the open-world erasure," and the observation of this trade-off has value in itself.

### The ablations are solid, but the fairness of the training data and backbone is questionable

The ablations are quite solid: MCCE's dropout probability, LD-CFG against MC-CFG and Simple Linear, the Locator/Preserver's step counts and data composition, the routing boundary, the VLM choice, incomplete prompts, and with vs. without mask are all swept, and the "same parameter count" Simple Linear control is a particular plus. But there are two fairness concerns. The first is the **training-data advantage**: DualEraser consumes about 100K pairs mixed from VOR + ROSE, whereas a baseline like ROSE is mainly trained on its own data and the backbones all differ, so part of the gain may come from "more data + a stronger Wan2.2/2.1 backbone" rather than the architecture itself; the paper does not provide a "move the baseline onto the Wan backbone and retrain on the same data" control. The second is that **VOR-Eval's PSNR is itself low and noisy**: all methods score only 21–24 dB on VOR-Eval, and the authors themselves measured VOR (Real) background MAE at 11.50, meaning the GT pairs are misaligned; on a benchmark with unclean GT, a 1.44 dB gap must have its signal strength discounted.

### The originality of the three modules is uneven

The novelty of the three modules is uneven. LD-CFG, the small module that turns CFG fusion into a learnable projection, is the most original, and has the Simple Linear control to support "it is not from more parameters." By contrast, Bipartite Text + MCCE is essentially "better prompt engineering + conditional dropout," and Locator–Preserver reuses Wan2.2's existing noise-level dual-expert with a different training objective/length/data—the authors also honestly admit the differences are only along these three dimensions, with the architecture reused. So rather than a brand-new architecture, it is better described as "one new small fusion module + a training recipe carefully designed for removal"; this is still useful to the community, but the "novel framework" wording is slightly inflated.

### Concerns about the VOR-Wild human-evaluation protocol and same-source split

The open-world headline (robust effect removal) rests almost entirely on VOR-Wild's two human preference rates, EPR/UPR, and the evaluation protocol warrants caution: EPR uses only **2** "video object removal experts" to rate 195 cases, and the paper does not state whether these two are independent of the authors; UPR uses only 5 volunteers, and each rates only a randomly assigned 117 of the cases, with each case rated by exactly 3 different volunteers (not all five rating all 195 cases), so the actual rating coverage is thinner than the surface headcount suggests. More critically, the "select multiple" protocol—raters may check multiple "successful" samples simultaneously—is looser than forced ranking, which tends to inflate scores overall and compress or amplify the gaps between methods, and neither statistical significance nor inter-rater agreement is reported. So a gap like 0.58 vs. 0.48 on EPR should be regarded as "directional evidence" rather than a hard conclusion. In addition, training uses ROSE-Train / VOR-Train and evaluation uses ROSE-Bench / VOR-Eval; the same-source splits have highly overlapping distributions, and although they are not literally the same data, there is still a risk of overfitting to "one's own distribution."

### Unresolved failure cases and reproducibility shortfalls

They are not fully resolved; the authors themselves report two kinds of failure: occlusion reconstruction (after removing a foreground horse, the horse occluded behind it has a broken structure) and physical causality (after removing a dribbling child, the basketball still copies the bounce trajectory originally driven by the child, violating physical causality, see Figure 9). This shows the model is still limited by the base model's generative capacity and the scarcity of complex interaction scenes in the training data. Reproducibility is the most practical shortfall: a transport-disabled static shallow clone (executing no code) of the official repo `cyqii/GenEraser` shows it tracks only three files—`README.md`, `LICENSE`, `asset/showcases/showcases.gif`—with **no inference code, config, or weights**, and the secret scan found nothing; the paper also gives no resolution, frame count, inference latency, or VRAM requirement. So apart from scattered numbers like "16×A100, batch 2, 40 steps, Wan backbone," faithfully reproducing it externally is currently not feasible. Furthermore, the main-table 5B ROSE PSNR is recorded as 33.33, but several appendix ablation tables (mask, VLM) instead give 33.55—an internal inconsistency within the paper, hinting that the headline table and the ablation tables may come from slightly different checkpoints/settings.

## One-minute version

- **Joint removal**: when erasing a video object, you must also clear its associated physical effects, to avoid leaving a contradictory frame of "the car is gone but the smoke remains." Example: after the water-surface boat is masked out, the model must not merely hollow out the boat body but also smooth over the spreading wake that falls outside the frame.
- **High/low-noise dual experts**: split the high-noise stage of "daring to delete the object" and the low-noise stage of "not moving the background" between two experts, Locator and Preserver, to break the contradiction that a single model, the longer it trains, dares less to erase effects. Example: a single model's open-world preference rate EPR falls from 0.4987 to 0.4346 by late training; after splitting, the high-noise expert focuses on erasure and the low-noise expert specializes in polishing background pixels on synthetic data.
- **Benchmark evaluation surpasses baselines**: on synthetic videos with ground-truth and open-world blind tests, both erasure quality and pixel-reconstruction metrics set new records over prior results. Example: the 5B model achieves 33.33 dB PSNR on the ROSE benchmark (beating the strongest competitor SVOR by 2.16 dB), and takes an open-world human erasure preference rate EPR of 0.5872.
- **Evaluation-substrate noise and rater bias**: the reliability of the leading numbers is limited by unaligned ground-truth and an overly loose subjective-annotation protocol. Example: the VOR real benchmark background error MAE as high as 11.50 means the ground-truth is itself unclean, while the open-world EPR is scored by only 2 raters who may multi-select, with statistical significance untested.
- **Open source is just a showcase shell**: the code repository the paper claims currently has no practical value, and external parties cannot reproduce it or put it into production at all. Example: the official GitHub project contains only documentation, a license, and one showcase GIF, and even the supported video resolution, frame rate, or inference VRAM requirement has never been published.
